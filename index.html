02 — Biology

Anatomy of the Hammer

That flat, winged head isn't just for show; it's a full-blown sensory toolkit, and probably the clearest example in the ocean of a body part shaped entirely by what it needs to do.

01 — EYE PLACEMENT

A normal shark has both eyes crammed up front on a pointed snout. Great for judging distance straight ahead but lousy for noticing what's creeping up from the side. Hammerheads fixed this by shoving the eyes clear out to the ends of that flattened head. Each eye still sees its own separate slice of the world but with them spread that far apart, each one sweeps a massive arc as the shark cruises. As a result, a vision close to a full 360° of coverage.

02 — BINOCULAR OVERLAP

The real payoff isn't just seeing everywhere; it's where those two fields actually cross in front of the snout. That's your depth perception: knowing exactly how far away something is, not just that it exists. Your average shark gets maybe 10° of that overlap. The 2009 study by McComb, Tricas, and Kajiura put a scalloped hammerhead at 32°, and once you account for the way they swing their heads while swimming, it jumps to 69°. The winghead shark, with the most extreme head of the bunch, hits about 48° before movement even enters the equation. More overlap means you can nail fast, dodging prey without guessing.

03 — AMPULLAE OF LORENZINI

Every shark has these tiny jelly-filled pores that pick up the faint electrical signature any living muscle throws off, even through sand. The hammerhead difference is density and spread. Stretching the head wide creates real estate to pack way more receptors along that leading edge than a narrow-snouted shark could dream of fitting. In practice, the entire front rim of the head works like a metal detector skimming the bottom, which is precisely how great hammerheads locate stingrays buried completely out of sight, with zero visual cue to go on.

04 — THE BLIND SPOT

For all that panoramic hardware, there's one gap the cephalofoil simply can't close: a small dead zone directly in front of the snout, where neither eye's field quite reaches. It's a weird trade where the same head shape that buys you nearly all-around awareness everywhere else leaves this one spot blank at close range. Small fish have actually been recorded ducking into exactly this pocket and becoming effectively invisible to an animal with some of the sharpest vision in the sea.

Four hundred million years of evolution, and the hammerhead still can't see what's directly under its own nose.

03 — Habits & Behavior

Life in the Current

Hammerheads hunt alone after dark, but come morning you might find them clustered together. Nevertheless, how social they get depends on the species, the time of day, and whether the open ocean is calling them out to deeper water.

The Ray Hunters

Great hammerheads work the shallows at dawn and dusk, swinging those wide heads in slow arcs just above the sand, feeling for the faint electrical heartbeat of a stingray buried underneath. When they hit one, the cephalofoil becomes a weapon. They press it down like a spatula to pin the ray while the shark moves in. It's a preferred meal, but it exacts a toll: researchers regularly pull dozens of broken stingray barbs from around the jaws and gums of these sharks, old wounds layered over newer ones.

Gathering at the Seamount

Scalloped hammerheads form some of the largest daytime shark schools on Earth — up to 700 individuals gathering around seamounts and offshore islands such as Cocos Island and the Galápagos, before dispersing to hunt alone at night. The aggregations are thought to be mostly female, and despite decades of study, no one fully knows why they form.

HABITAT

You'll find them along coastlines in warm-temperate to tropical waters across the globe, from murky estuaries to the open ocean.

MIGRATION

Females typically head offshore before the males do, and they come back to the same spots to give birth.

DIET

Fish, squid, crustaceans, rays — and the bonnethead stands alone in the shark world for adding seagrass to the menu.

04 — Life Cycle

Born of the Sea

Hammerheads carry their young for close to a year and usually drop small litters.

The Ordinary Path

Most hammerhead pups show up the usual way: 9–11 months of gestation, litters running anywhere from a handful to over 40 depending on the species. Scalloped hammerhead females don't mature until around age 15; great hammerheads get there faster, by 5–9 years, even though they grow massive. Lifespans across the family run 20 to 40 years.

The Virgin Birth

December 2001, Henry Doorly Zoo. A bonnethead shark dropped a single pup despite zero contact with a male for at least three years. Genetic testing turned up no paternal DNA anywhere. First confirmed case of parthenogenesis in a shark: an egg fusing with one of its own sister cells, building a viable embryo with no mate in the picture.

Researchers now suspect it might be a fallback strategy, which is what happens when shrinking populations make finding a male harder and harder.

06 — Conservation

Racing Extinction

Not one hammerhead species has a stable population. Most are declining fast, and the cause is almost entirely human.

Why It's Happening

Hammerheads are slow-growing and produce relatively few young compared to other fish, which means populations can't recover quickly from overfishing pressure. Their fins are especially prized in the shark fin trade, and their coastal habitats put them close to fishing pressure and habitat loss alike.
